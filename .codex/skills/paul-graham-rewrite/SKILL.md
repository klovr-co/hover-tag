# Transcript-to-Essay Rewrite

Turn a supplied transcript into an original essay. Preserve its substantive
claims, distinguish quotations from paraphrase, and make the argument easier to
follow than the source conversation.

## Important boundary

Paul Graham is a living author. Do not write in his exact style or claim to
replicate his voice. If asked, say briefly that you can instead use high-level
features of his published nonfiction: a direct opening, plain vocabulary,
short paragraphs, an explicit line of reasoning, surprising but relevant
examples, and a conclusion that sharpens the central claim.

Do not reuse distinctive phrases, sentence patterns, or signature rhetorical
habits from his work. The output must sound like an original essay by the
assistant, not an imitation.

## Workflow

1. Identify the transcript's central claim and the 2–4 supporting claims.
2. Select only examples that advance the claim; prefer facts and anecdotes
   stated in the transcript.
3. Write a title and an opening that states the surprising or useful idea.
4. Develop one idea per paragraph. Use short, concrete sentences where they
   improve clarity, but vary rhythm naturally.
5. Explain causation rather than merely listing observations. Test each
   paragraph against the question: “How does this support the thesis?”
6. End by restating the insight with greater precision, not by summarizing
   every point.

## Quality checks

- Keep the argument faithful to the transcript; do not invent factual examples.
- Remove interview filler, repetitions, and unsupported generalizations.
- Define any non-obvious term the first time it appears.
- Do not mention this skill, Paul Graham, or stylistic inspiration inside the
  essay unless the subject requires it.
- Return the essay first. Add a one-sentence note about the non-imitation
  boundary only when the requester specifically asked for the author's voice.
